Sightline Verdict · Redacted Client · fixed-scope diagnostic

Sample verdict document

This is an illustrative deliverable — anonymized, redacted, and shortened. It shows what a Sightline engagement ends with: not a bug list, but a map of where promises are at risk.

Supported living provider (Midwest). Names, URLs, and vendor details removed. Content is representative, not a live client file.

01 — The promise

What the organization believed the system was doing

Families and care coordinators could reach out through the website when someone needed a residential placement.

The contact form on the homepage was treated as the front door for new intakes. Marketing copy promised a response within one business day. Staff assumed every submission landed in the CRM. Leadership believed the digital intake path matched what families were told on the phone.

02 — Exposure surface

Where promises are at risk

An exposure surface is a map of where the system's promises might fail — sorted by urgency, not by which team owns the fix.

In plain terms

Think of it like a weather map for trust: some spots need action now, some need watching, and some are holding.

Tier Finding Why it matters
Urgent Homepage contact form failing silently — expired third-party API key Every submission since key expiry discarded with no error shown to the visitor and no alert to staff
Watch No error monitoring on form submission path Next failure would repeat the same silent gap — no one would know until intake volume dropped
Watch Success message still displays after failed submission Visitors believe help is on the way; organization believes silence means low demand
Watch No documented backup intake path on the site Phone number exists but is not surfaced as fallback when digital intake fails
Stable Phone intake line staffed and logged in CRM Human path works — but volume is low because marketing drives families to the form

03 — Seams named

Where interpretation authority changed hands

These are the handoffs where meaning got lost — not because anyone failed, but because no one owned the seam.

  • → Original developer departed — no one retained backend access or API credentials documentation
  • → Marketing owns front-end copy and form UX; operations owns CRM — neither monitors submission delivery
  • → Form plugin vendor rotated API keys; renewal notice went to a departed employee's inbox
  • → "The site works" tested visually, not functionally — green checkmark on publish masked broken intake

04 — Verdict

What you can't un-see

One paragraph. The diagnostic question answered.

The system did not keep the promise it made. Families were told they could reach out online and expect a response. For months, the form looked fine while every submission vanished — and because nothing alerted anyone, the organization interpreted silence as low demand rather than broken intake. The risk isn't one expired key. It's that no seam between marketing, web, and operations was owned enough to catch it.

BDR-0005

05 — Recommended sequence

What to fix first — and what to watch

Not a build proposal. A priority order so fixes don't scatter.

  1. Restore and verify form delivery — rotate API credentials, submit test intakes, confirm CRM receipt
  2. Fix the success-state lie — form must not thank the visitor when submission failed
  3. Add submission monitoring — alert on failure, not on silence
  4. Surface backup intake on the site — phone path visible when digital path is primary marketing message
  5. Assign seam ownership — one role accountable for "does intake still work?" quarterly

Full Sightline engagements include facilitated readout and seam documentation. This sample shows the written artifact only.
